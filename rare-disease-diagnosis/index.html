Semantics of Missingness in Medical AI: A Case Study in Rare-Disease Diagnosis with "No X"

Raj Hansini, Cyrus Zhou

November 12, 2026

A Patient Journey

Rare-disease patients often see many doctors before diagnosis. Each doctor checks for the disease they suspect and records negative findings as stated absences, such as "no lens dislocation"; we call these "no X" (Figure 1).

Current diagnosis tools treat every "no X" as evidence against the diseases that have X. Yet in Figure 1, the pediatrician recorded "no lens dislocation" because they suspected Marfan, the correct diagnosis. Like an ordered heart scan, a "no X" reveals what the doctor had in mind. We ask whether it can serve as a clue for the diagnosis.

That's where our work comes in. We use Phenopacket Store, 4,782 published rare-disease case reports, each listing the patient's symptoms as Human Phenotype Ontology (HPO) terms and the confirmed diagnosis. Diagnosis tools look up each disease's symptoms in a database curated from many of these same papers, so a tool could get a case right because it has already seen it, a problem known as data leakage. We therefore report results only on leak-free cases.

In most leak-free cases, at least one "no X" is a known feature of the true disease (Figure 5). Absences mark where the doctor was looking, so penalizing them pushes the correct diagnosis down.

We evaluated LIRICAL (Robinson et al., AJHG 2020), the standard likelihood-ratio tool for rare-disease diagnosis, and our scorer, a simplified re-implementation for controlled experiments (Table 1). Counting "no X" as a weak clue instead of a rule-out, a one-term change, raised top-10 accuracy for both (Figure 6).

Every LLM we tested performs better when the absences are kept (Figure 7), but its ranking does not show how each absence was used. Our categorizer has the LLM sort each candidate disease's symptoms into evidence buckets that a formula then weighs (Figure 9). It raised the top-10 accuracy of Gemini 3.5 Flash (Figure 11), and the learned weight for absent symptoms was positive.

Exomiser 14.0.1, a widely used tool that ranks genes and diseases by matching a patient's HPO terms to known disease phenotypes, has a parsing bug that reads "no X" as X. The bug raises its top-10 accuracy (Figure 8), independent evidence that "no X" points to the right disease. Reese et al. (EJHG 2026) compared LLMs against this version, so its Exomiser scores include the bug's effect.

What doctors mean by "no"

Absences do not all mean the same thing. We distinguish three kinds (Figure 2); the first matches the GA4GH Phenopackets definition of an excluded feature, "looked for, but found to be absent".

Evaluation setup

Note: This section covers the data, tools and scoring; skip it to go straight to the results.

Data. Of Phenopacket Store's 4,782 cases, 2,701 (56.5%) are leaked: the HPO annotation for the true disease cites the case's own paper. All main results use leak-free cases (Table 2), of which 1,172 have at least one "no X". Some experiments use slightly fewer; each figure gives its count.

Systems. Three symbolic tools that score diseases with a fixed formula (LIRICAL 2.4.1, our scorer, Exomiser 14.0.1) and six LLMs (GPT-5, GPT-5-mini, Gemini 3.5 Flash, Claude Sonnet 5, DeepSeek V4.1 Flash, GPT-4o).

Metric. Top-10 accuracy: the fraction of cases whose true disease ranks in the top ten.

How LIRICAL scores a disease. For each candidate disease D, LIRICAL multiplies one likelihood ratio per symptom:

S(D)=∏X∈PfD(X)fbg(X)×∏X∈A1−fD(X)1−fbg(X)(1)
  • P: symptoms the patient has
  • A: stated absences ("no X")
  • fD(X): fraction of patients with disease D who have X
  • fbg(X): how common X is across all diseases (definitions per tool in Table 1)
  • Each "no X" multiplies a disease that has X by a factor below one, so it always lowers that disease (Figure 3, Figure 4)

Our scorer. A simplified LIRICAL with the same two likelihood ratios, built to run controlled experiments and inspect each step; it is a baseline, not a contribution. Both apply Equation 1, so our modification applies equally to LIRICAL. Table 1 lists the differences.

Results

Our main findings:

  • Reading "no X" as a Clue Turns the Loss into a Gain. We replaced the rule-out ratio in Equation 1 with a weak positive ratio, giving Equation 2:
S(D)=∏X∈PfD(X)fbg(X)×∏X∈A(fD(X)fbg(X))α(2)
  • α (0.1 to 0.5 across folds) weights an absence below an observed symptom and is chosen by 5-fold cross-validation grouped by paper (Appendix). Results are in Figure 6.
  • Symbolic Tools Lose When Given "no X", but Every LLM Gains. We ran each system with the absences deleted and kept (Figure 7). The symbolic tools get worse and every LLM gets better, so the signal is real and the symbolic tools misread it.
  • Exomiser's Parsing Bug Produces the Same Effect. Run in phenotype-only mode, Exomiser 14.0.1 ignores the excluded flag in Phenopacket v2 files (GitHub issue #450), scoring each "no X" as an observed X, which raises its top-10 accuracy (Figure 8). Ranks for the 1,316 cases without excluded terms are unchanged, so the gain comes entirely from excluded terms. Reese et al. (EJHG 2026) used this version as their LLM baseline. We are reporting the issue to the maintainers.

For LLMs: The Categorizer

LLMs have no explicit formula to correct, so the categorizer has the LLM record its evidence and a simple formula produce the ranking, in three steps (Figure 9).

The learned weight for absent symptoms is positive and nearly as large as that for present symptoms (Figure 9; buckets for the running example in Figure 10).

We also let the LLM rank the candidates itself instead of the formula, and compared both with its first guess (Figure 11).

Conclusion: Where Do We Go From Here?

Our findings bring bad news and good news.

The bad news is that standard symbolic tools read "no X" as a rule-out, pushing the right diagnosis down, while a widely used tool reads it as present by accident and served as a published LLM baseline (Reese et al., EJHG 2026). The good news is that the fix is simple: reading "no X" as a clue helps every system we tested (Figure 12).

Absence has no agreed meaning: the Phenopacket excluded field means "looked for and found absent" in the standard, a rule-out to LIRICAL, and present to Exomiser 14.0.1. We see four directions for future research (Figure 13).

Appendix

Data leakage

  • Our scorer, top-10: 82.5% on leaked cases vs 33.2% on clean cases.
  • Excluded terms annotated as present for the true disease: 95.9% (leaked) vs 30.5% (clean).
  • Same-paper leakage has been reported before (Jiang et al. 2026; Yang et al. 2026); excluded terms were not tested.
  • 457 cases have no PubMed ID and are excluded from both groups.

Choosing α

  • 5-fold cross-validation grouped by paper. α per fold: our scorer 0.5, 0.5, 0.5, 0.5, 0.3; LIRICAL 0.1, 0.3, 0.1, 0.1, 0.1.
  • Held-out half (521 cases), α fixed on the other half: top-10 31.5% → 39.9%.
  • Control, same number of random terms from other papers: 30.3%.
  • 13 softened rule-out penalties: best 42.1%, below ignoring "no X" (43.5%).
  • Top-1 accuracy: no improvement.
